## A tear-off is designed to destroy the stack

Ask a roofer to take a roof off and you get a tear-off: crews on the deck with shovels, every
layer scraped into a dumpster, the sheathing pried up behind it. It is fast, it is well understood,
and it is the correct answer when the only goal is an empty deck.

But look at what it does to the assembly. A roof is not a thing — it is a **stack**. Rafters went
up first, sheathing over them, underlayment over that, shingles last. Every layer was fastened
through the layer beneath it. A tear-off works down through that stack from the top, in the same
direction the fasteners were driven, which means every layer is destroyed on the way to the one
below it. The shingles come off in fragments. The underlayment tears. The sheathing splits on the
nails that hold it. The rafters take the prying.

At the end you have a clear deck and a full dumpster, and the assembly that a previous crew spent
days building is now debris. **The material value recovered is zero — and the homeowner paid for
the destruction.**

Deconstruction asks a different question: *can the stack come apart at its seams instead of through
them?* Everything below follows from that.

## Why the roof goes first

Construction builds bottom-up. Deconstruction runs top-down — and the roof leads for the same
reasons it went on last.

It is the most weather-exposed assembly on the building and the one whose condition varies most.
It is the highest work, so it carries the most fall exposure. And it sits over everything else, so
nothing below it can be worked in controlled conditions until it is gone.

Take the roof down to grade and the rest of the house becomes ordinary work at ordinary heights.
Leave it up and every phase underneath it inherits the roof's risk.

## Brace before you cut

This is the step that gets skipped, and it is the one that matters most.

**A roof is not just a cover — it is a diaphragm.** It ties the tops of the exterior walls together
and braces them against racking. Remove it and the top-floor walls lose their restraint. Exterior
walls can bow outward; the structure loses the thing that was holding it square.

So the bracing goes in **before the first cut is made**, not after the roof is off:

- Horizontal kickers across the top floor, corner to corner
- Diagonal bracing at intervals along the exterior walls, top plate to floor
- A check that no header over an opening was depending on the roof above it

And the bracing comes out **last** — during the wall phase, not before it. The order is not a
formality. Between the release cut and the finished bracing there is no version of this job that
is safe.

The rest of the pre-work is assessment: how the rafters actually tie to the wall plate, which
interior walls are carrying the ridge, whether the framing is stick-built or trusses, where the
chimney and vents interrupt the field, how steep the pitch is, and whether any sheathing is rotted
enough to fail under load during a lift. Each answer changes the cut plan. None of them can be
answered from the ground.

## Cut at the rafter, not between it

To move a roof in pieces, the pieces have to survive being moved. A section that flexes across its
span will fold when it is picked up.

So the cut lines follow the framing:

1. **The ridge first** — down through the ridge cap and the ridge board, separating the two planes.
2. **Along rafter lines, cutting *at* a rafter rather than between two of them.** This is the whole
   technique. A section cut at the rafters keeps a full rafter along each edge, and those edge
   rafters are what make the section rigid enough to lift as a unit. A section cut through the bay
   between rafters is a piece of sheathing with no spine.
3. **The rafter-to-plate connection last.** This is the release cut — the moment the section stops
   being part of the building.

Which is why the rigging is attached **before** the release cut, never after. A section that has
been freed from the structure and is not yet held by the crane is a section nobody controls.

Sections get sized so each one stays well inside the lift plan, and the first one comes off a gable
end where there is a free edge to work from. Hips, dormers and steep pitches all complicate the
plan and all take longer than a straightforward gable.

## The flip is the whole trick

Here is the part that makes the method work.

Sections come off the building the way they sat on it: shingles up, rafters down. In that
orientation, everything you want to disassemble is on the underside — you would be working
overhead, prying upward, fighting gravity for every fastener, exactly as a tear-off does.

**So the section gets turned over.** Rafters up, shingles down.

And it is turned over deliberately, not dropped. The crane sets the section down on **one long
edge** first — standing it on its side, still under the hook, with the ground crew steadying it
rather than carrying it. From there the crane rolls it across that edge and lays it down inverted.
The rotation happens about a line that is already on the ground, so the section is never
free-falling and never unsupported: it pivots, it does not flop. A section small enough to handle
can have the last part of the roll finished by hand once the crane has set it.

The landing zone is prepared before any of this — a cleared, level footprint with ground protection
laid down, so the shingle face comes to rest on a cushion instead of bare grade. The point of the
whole maneuver is to preserve layers; landing the assembly on rubble would undo it in one move.

Now look at what the flip actually did. The rafter face is the **inside** of the assembly — the
face that looked into the attic, the side nobody could reach while the roof was a roof. Turning the
section over puts that inside face up. So the work runs **from the inside out**: you start at the
surface that was built first and work outward toward the surface that was built last.

Worth being precise here, because it is easy to get backwards. A tear-off is what runs in
reverse-construction order — shingles first, because shingles went on last. The flipped section is
worked the other way: rafters first, in the same order the roof was *built*, but reached from the
inside. **The house comes apart in reverse. Each assembly in it comes apart inside out.** Those two
directions are opposites, and both are correct at their own scale.

That is the difference between this and a tear-off. A tear-off attacks the assembly from the
outside in, breaking *through* each layer to reach the next one. Working inside out means each
layer is **freed** rather than broken — released from the layer beneath it while both are still
whole. Every separation happens at the **joint between two layers** instead of through the face of
one: from the rafter side you can reach the rafter-to-sheathing connection directly and cut the
fasteners where they cross it, releasing both pieces whole. From the shingle side you could only
dig down through the shingles to find that same connection, destroying both on the way. And it all
runs *downward*, with gravity helping instead of resisting.

**The goal is to free every layer, not to remove the roof.** Removing the roof is the easy part and
a machine can do it in an hour. Freeing four layers so that all four survive is the actual work, and
the flip is what makes it possible.

The safety consequence is bigger than the recovery one. Inverting the section moves nearly all of
the disassembly labor **from height to grade**. The work that used to be a full day of tie-off on
a pitched surface becomes a short, controlled period of rigging exposure, and everything after it
happens at ground level with a clear footprint around it.

## Layer by layer

With the section inverted, the stack comes apart from the inside out — innermost layer first,
outermost layer last:

**1. Rafters.** The innermost layer, now on top and fully accessible. The fasteners at the sheathing connection get cut
rather than pulled, and the rafters lift away clean and dry. Recovered rafters are the highest-value
material in the assembly — especially in older housing stock, where full-dimension old-growth
framing is simply not something you can buy new.

**2. Sheathing.** Freed from the rafters, the panels separate from the underlayment beneath. This
is the layer most sensitive to technique: plywood tolerates more than OSB, and both split if the
fasteners are fought instead of removed.

**3. Underlayment.** Synthetic underlayment peels cleanly and is reusable when it comes up intact.
Self-adhered ice-and-water shield does not — it is bonded to the sheathing by design, and it comes
off as waste.

**4. Shingles.** The outermost layer, and the last one reached. Already at the bottom, against the
ground, with nothing fastened through them any more — every fastener that held them was removed
from above, in turn, on the way down. They release downward and stay together instead of shattering
into the fragments a shovel produces.

Each layer is freed before the layer beneath it is disturbed. That is the entire discipline: four
layers, released one at a time, all four intact — instead of one deck cleared and four layers lost.

## Ring-shank nails are the real adversary

If one detail decides whether this method recovers material or merely relocates it, it is the
fastener.

**Ring-shank nails are engineered specifically not to withdraw.** The rings are there to resist
exactly the force you would apply to pull one. Fight them and something gives: pry from the
sheathing side and the panel splits; pry from the framing side and the rafter takes the damage.
Either way you trade one recovered material for the other.

The alternative is not to pull at all. **Cut the fastener** — flush, between the rafter and the
sheet — and the two layers separate without either one absorbing the load. The stub stays put and
gets driven flush later. It costs a couple of minutes per sheet.

That trade is worth naming, because it is the clearest possible illustration of how ML Systems
makes decisions. Spending a small, deliberate cost to protect a much larger recoverable value is
**MVE — Minimum Viable Expense**: not the cheapest option in the moment, but the one that pays back
along the most axes at once.

Cut the nail, keep the rafter *and* the sheet. Pull the nail, keep one of them. Pry the panel, keep
neither.

## Safety is the constraint, not the footnote

Everything above sits inside a safety envelope, and the envelope is what shapes the method:

- **Fall protection** for anyone on the roof until it is off — full tie-off, rated anchors, no
  unprotected edge work. `OSHA 1926 Subpart M`
- **A written lift plan** reviewed by the operator and the rigger before the first pick, with a
  barricaded swing radius, full outrigger extension on firm ground, and hard limits on power-line
  clearance. `OSHA 1926 Subpart CC`
- **A wind limit.** A roof section is a large flat plate — it is a sail long before it is heavy.
- **One voice to the crane.** The rigger signals; nobody else does.
- **Silica and dust control** when cutting. `OSHA 1926.1153`
- **Ground discipline** below: magnetic sweeps on a schedule, hard hats during every flip, and
  rotation between cutting and carrying so nobody does the same load-bearing motion all day.

The reason the flip matters so much is visible in this list. Most of these controls exist to manage
*working at height*. Move the disassembly to grade and you are not managing that risk better — you
have removed most of it.

## What the roof teaches the system

A deconstruction is not only a recovery. It is a **measurement**, and the roof is the first place
the system gets to check its beliefs about a house against the house itself.

Everything the crew observes — how the sections actually behaved under load, what each layer
yielded, which fastener strategy worked on this vintage of framing — becomes structured data:

- **Ontology rows.** Each assembly is described in the shared grammar of the
  [Collective Ontology](../../docs/collective-ontology.md), so the roofer, the estimator, the model
  and eventually the robot all describe the same rafter the same way.
- **Ledger claims.** Each recovered assembly posts a claim to the
  [Master Ledger](../../docs/master-ledger.md), carrying its source and evidence grade — then gets
  reconciled against what the assessor's record, the homeowner's memory and the model's prediction
  each said was up there. Roofs are where those three disagree most often.
- **A compressed record.** What is learned collapses into the home's HomeGenome — the mechanism
  [Ontological Compression](../../docs/ontological-compression.md) uses to reduce a whole house to
  something a model can reason over.
- **A reverse takeoff.** REAPER, the deconstruction mind among the
  [Seven Minds](../../docs/the-seven-minds.md), compiles the bill of materials and routes every line
  by **RRR — Reuse › Resale › Recycle**, to its most valuable recoverable state.

Each recovered material carries an **ML Material ID** and a public provenance record — a grade, a
contamination status, and a chain of custody — so a buyer can check what they are getting and a
regulator can check what was diverted.

**The roof is where the ontology gets its ground truth.** The prediction was made from an assessor
record, a satellite image and a street-level photo. The roof is the first time the building answers
back.

## The honest part

This is a method, not a result.

**No ML Systems deconstruction has been performed yet.** Everything described here is a designed
sequence — reasoned from framing practice, from the safety standards it has to satisfy, and from
what the material is worth if it survives. It is not a report from a completed job, and the
recovery figures modeled elsewhere in this system are targets rather than measurements.

The method will change once a real roof argues with it. That first argument is the point — it is
the ground truth the whole system is built to go collect.
