## The weekend the frontier agreed to slow down

On Saturday, September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei published an essay called We Must Pace the Frontier. The sentence everyone quoted was this one: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain."

He was not asking for a pause. He said so. Not building the technology, in his words, "deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers." What he asked for was pace: a three-part plan — embedded evaluators inside the labs with employee-level access, coordination among the democratic labs on common safety standards and limits on unchecked capability growth, and eventually global coordination on the most dangerous capabilities, including a speed limit on models building the next generation of models. Anthropic committed to the first step unilaterally, that day.

By evening Sam Altman had written "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier," and said OpenAI would do the same on evaluators. Elon Musk wrote three words: "Dario is right." Three people who agree on almost nothing agreed on this.

I read it at my kitchen table in Rhode Island, and I did not feel vindicated. I felt like someone had finally said out loud, in public, the thing that had wrecked me a year earlier.

## A year ago, at the same table

About a year ago I had what I can only call an existential crisis about speed. Not about machines becoming smart — I build with them every day and I like them. It was about the gap. Capability was compounding. Human understanding — mine, my neighbors', the town clerk's, the carpenter's — was not. It grows the way it always has: one person, one conversation, one mistake at a time. Two curves, one exponential and one linear, and the distance between them is where people get hurt. Not in a science-fiction way. In a your-lender-uses-a-model-you-can't-question way. In a your-house-got-torn-down-because-a-spreadsheet-said-so way.

I could not slow the frontier. Nobody can from Warwick. What I could do was choose one place where that gap would land on ordinary people first, and build the slow lane there on purpose.

So on December 3, 2025, I formed Machine Learning Systems LLC. Not a lab. A construction company — NAICS 236115 — with a software layer. The name is deliberate. If machine learning systems are going to reshape wealth in this country, I wanted at least one of them to be pointed at the oldest, most ordinary form of wealth there is: the house a family already owns.

## The American Dream, attributed correctly

The American Dream has a unit, and the unit is a home. Not a portfolio — a home, with equity in it that a family built by living there and paying it down.

The industry that serves that dream has a strange habit. When a house needs to become something more, we destroy it. Demolition turns a structure that took a hundred people to assemble into a landfill line item in a day. Every rafter that could have carried the next roof, every board that could have been resold to the family down the street, goes into a container. Then the same family borrows to buy new material to build the thing they just threw away.

ML Systems exists to replace that habit with a different one: deconstruction in lieu of demolition. Take the house apart in reverse build order. Route every recovered material to its most valuable recoverable state — reuse it in the rebuild, resell it at a builder's open house, recycle what is left. Then rebuild the home from its own materials — larger, with near-zero waste — and let the equity that creates belong to the people who already lived there. The loop closes when the homeowner chooses to keep building. That choice, not a projection, is the flywheel.

None of this is a report from a completed job. No ML Systems deconstruction has been performed yet. The 80–90% recovery target is MODELED. The two-day crane sequence is ASPIRATIONAL. What is MEASURED is the software: a shipped app, a working ledger, and an ontology that seven agents and one human speak fluently. I say which is which every time, because the whole point is that a person can check.

## Pace is not a feeling. It is a design decision.

Here is what I learned building it: you cannot slow a system down by asking it nicely. You slow it down by deciding, in the grammar, who is allowed to say what is true.

So the system does not store facts. It stores claims. Every entry on a home's Master Ledger holds claims — who said it, what evidence grade backs it, what value they gave — and claims are reconciled by domain: the homeowner leads on the interior, the record leads on the legal card, VERA leads on what a satellite can see. Every mind claims. Five seats ground. VERA gates. Only the Custodian stamps. The Custodian is me, a person, and my stamp is bound to a content hash; change the content and the signature lapses.

My AI agents do not talk to each other. They talk to a ledger, and a human reads the ledger. A mind may claim; it may never ground. That sentence is the slow lane. It means the fastest model in the world can propose a roof height, and it does not become a roof height until a homeowner with a tape measure or an assessor's record or a verifier's read stands beside it — and a disagreement between credible sources is quarantined, not averaged.

When I read Dario's first step — evaluators embedded inside the lab with employee-level access — I recognized it. That is what the seats are. The homeowner, the town record, the verifier and the Custodian are embedded in every output the agents produce, with the same access the agents have, and nothing leaves without them. I did not design it to match an essay. I designed it because a year ago I was scared, and the only thing that made the fear useful was writing down who gets to say what a house is.

## MVE: the balance in MVP

Every startup is told to build an MVP. In American English that acronym means two things at once, and they pull in opposite directions. Most Valuable Player is about being the most. Minimum Viable Product is about being the least that still works. Nobody ever tells you how to hold both.

I run the company — and the agents — on a third term that is the balance of the two: MVE, Minimum Viable Expense. It is not the cheapest option in the moment. It is the one expense that is both the smallest that works and the most valuable thing you could have spent on, because it returns more than once. In the deconstruction lab, a single measured spend returns four ways: the recovered material's value, the ontology data the job produces, the training signal that data becomes, and the market intelligence about what a Rhode Island house is actually made of. The agents are instructed under the same lens. An orchestrator that burns compute to look impressive is not the most valuable player; it is the most expensive one. Pace, in money terms, is MVE.

That lens — Transparency Trust, the Lucent Lens, Minimum Viable Expense — is the whole operating philosophy in three words, and it is the same answer to the same fear. Do not profit from the speed. Optimize it, and point it at a person.

## What I am asking

I am not asking the frontier to wait for a carpenter in Warwick. Dario is right, and Sam is right to join him, and the three steps are the right steps. I am asking something smaller and closer to the ground: that while the labs pace the frontier, the rest of us pace the arrival. Build the systems that will meet ordinary people at their front door so that a human name is on every record, so that the money moves at the speed of a family's decision and not a model's, and so that the first thing a machine learning system does to a house is keep it out of the landfill.

That is why the company has the name it has. That is what I built with the year the fear gave me. If the frontier really does buy itself another year or two, this is what I would ask people to do with it.
